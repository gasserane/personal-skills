# Rights-holder coverage pass

Read this during the programmatic-substance pass (SKILL.md Step 3), not as a final tick-through. It is a reading lens, not a checklist to run at the end.

## Why this exists

On a real CERV run, four of the nine substantive areas the donor raised and the scoring missed sat in this single category. Child rights alone appeared in all three of the donor criteria and was missed in all three. The instruction was visible in the proposal itself, on a page that had been read. It was read as an ethics instruction and never converted into a scoring lens.

Donors working in rights, equality and civil-society funding apply these dimensions as standard, whether or not the call names them. Several carry a dedicated status field on the evaluation form, which means an evaluator has to form a view on them even if the applicant never raised them.

## The four-level test

For every group below, ask four questions in order. Each level is a different finding at a different criterion.

| Level | Question | Where it usually scores |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Named | Does the group appear at all, in target groups or needs? | Relevance |
| 2. Specified | Is the approach for this group described, or only the group listed? | Relevance and Quality |
| 3. Tracked | Does any indicator, disaggregation or evaluation question cover it? | Quality |
| 4. Reached | Is there specific outreach, accessibility or engagement design? | Impact |

A group named at level 1 and absent at levels 2 to 4 is a finding, not coverage. This is the pattern donors describe as "mentioned, but the proposal lacks detail on how".

## The groups

### Children, and the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child

The most commonly missed and the most commonly scored. Ask:

- Is the Convention on the Rights of the Child **cited by name**? Its absence is flagged specifically by some donors.
- Are **age bands** given for children and adolescents in education, outreach or service activities? "Young people" covering 10 to 30 is not an age band.
- Is the approach described as **child-sensitive**, with what that means operationally?
- Is child rights in the **monitoring and evaluation framework**, particularly for youth activities, or only in the safeguarding annex?
- Is safeguarding **enforced and monitored across partner organisations**, or only adopted by the lead applicant? Adoption and enforcement are different claims and donors ask for the second.
- Is there **child-specific outreach**, designed for accessibility and engagement rather than assumed through existing networks?

### Adolescents and age bands

Distinct from children. Adolescence spans very different needs at 10, 15 and 19. Ask whether the design differentiates, whether consent and confidentiality provisions vary by age, and whether the indicators disaggregate by age band rather than reporting a single youth figure.

### Disability

Ask whether disability appears beyond an accessibility sentence. Is there a measurement approach — the Washington Group Short Set is the standard where disaggregation is claimed. Are physical, digital and communication accessibility all addressed, or only venue access. Is disability in the risk register as a barrier to participation.

### Refugees, migrants and displaced people

Frequently named in a target-population list and absent from the design. Ask whether interventions are **tailored** for this group, whether language and legal-status barriers are addressed, and whether the needs assessment covers them at all.

### Rural populations

The quiet omission. Programmes concentrated in capitals and large cities often claim national reach. Ask where activities actually happen, whether digital delivery assumes connectivity, and whether transport, distance or service availability appear anywhere in the barriers analysis.

### Roma and other minoritised ethnic groups

Ask whether the group is named or covered only by "marginalised communities". Ask whether representative organisations are partners rather than beneficiaries. Ask whether the needs assessment drew on them.

### LGBTIQ people

Ask whether the design distinguishes sexual orientation, gender identity and expression, and sex characteristics, or collapses them. Ask whether the context analysis covers legal and safety conditions in restrictive environments, and whether do-no-harm provisions follow from that.

## Cross-cutting questions

Three questions cut across every group and each is a donor formulation seen in practice:

1. **Whose voices produced the needs assessment?** Is there explicit evidence that marginalised communities were **consulted** in producing it, rather than described in it. Judging needs evidence on source quality alone misses this entirely.
2. **Is the data disaggregated?** A needs assessment citing strong sources but reporting only totals is a finding.
3. **Are intersections worked through?** Not parallel lists of groups, but how intersecting identities affect access. This connects to the lens-substantiveness test in SKILL.md Step 4.

## Emerging dimensions

Donors increasingly raise these unprompted, and they are easy to miss because they are not traditional rights-holder categories:

- **Digital safety and AI risk mitigation**, especially where the programme itself uses digital delivery or names AI as a work-stream. Absence weakens both sustainability and inclusiveness in donor language.
- **Localised approaches**: whether the design adapts to national and sub-national context, or applies one model everywhere.
- **Engagement with men and boys** where the programme targets gender equality. A narrative work-stream aimed at men is not the same as an engagement strategy for reaching them.
- **Barriers to collaboration in restrictive environments**, where partners operate under legal or political pressure.
