# Brief: should the MA extend longer clinic hours to all eight districts?

**Recommendation: do not extend to all eight districts yet. Extend to two or three more districts in 2026, keep two or three as comparison, and find out why District B did not respond.** The pilot results split. District A grew far more than the non-pilot district, and District B grew far less, so the data cannot show that longer hours caused the growth.

## What the numbers show

| District | 2024 | 2025 | Change | Growth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A (pilot) | 1,240 | 1,610 | +370 | 29.8% |
| B (pilot) | 860 | 905 | +45 | 5.2% |
| C (no pilot) | 1,020 | 1,190 | +170 | 16.7% |
| Both pilot districts | 2,100 | 2,515 | +415 | 19.8% |

I recomputed these figures from the table. The data are illustrative.

1. **Client numbers rose everywhere.** The mid-2025 contraception campaign ran in all three districts, so part of every increase probably comes from the campaign, not from opening hours. District C had no new hours and still grew by about one sixth.
2. **The fair test is pilot growth against District C growth.** Pooled, the pilot districts grew 19.8% against 16.7% in C, a gap of about 3 percentage points. At C's growth rate, the two pilot districts would have reached about 2,450 clients. They reached 2,515, so roughly 65 extra clients may be linked to longer hours.
3. **The two pilot districts point in opposite directions.** District A beat C by about 13 percentage points. District B fell about 11 points below C. The pooled result hides this split.

## Why the evidence does not yet support all eight districts

An MA reader can fairly say: "District A grew by 30%. Why wait?" The answer is that one district cannot carry a decision for eight. If A has something special, such as a large student population or a strong youth network, longer hours elsewhere may not reproduce its result. If B is the typical case, extending to all eight districts would add staff costs for little gain.

Two further limits apply. We have no data for earlier years, so we do not know whether A was already growing faster. We also count clients, not new clients or repeat visits, so the figures cannot show whether longer hours reached young people who stayed away before.

## What the wider evidence says

The published evidence supports making services more youth-friendly as a package. It does not isolate opening hours.

- WHO and UNAIDS (2015) set global standards for adolescent care that call for services adolescents can reach without an appointment, at low cost, with privacy protected. The summary page does not mention opening hours.
- WHO (2012) describes youth-friendly services in terms of quality and helps governments set national standards. The summary page does not list opening hours as a defined dimension.
- Chandra-Mouli and colleagues (2016) reviewed government efforts in eight low- and middle-income countries. Quality improved, and use by adolescents rose in seven of the eight countries, but only with support. The authors call the evidence modest, because study quality varied and some studies had no comparison sites. The paper does not test opening hours. One Indonesian assessment in it rated "services provided outside regular hours" at only 9%, which shows that hours are a common gap.

This evidence fits a cautious step. Hours are a plausible barrier, but no source we opened shows that extended hours alone raise youth attendance.

## Practical plan for 2026

1. **Find out why B lagged.** Ask District B staff and young clients in the first month. Check whether evening and Saturday sessions were publicised, staffed consistently, and safe to reach after dark.
2. **Pick two or three new districts and keep two or three as comparison.** Choose them with managers, and write down why, so the comparison stays fair.
3. **Record the same figures monthly.** Count new young clients, repeat visits, and clients who came in the extended hours, split by age and gender.
4. **Decide in mid-2026.** If the new districts beat the comparison districts, extend to the rest. If not, redirect the funds to what the B inquiry finds.

## The strongest objection

Waiting costs a year, and young people in five districts keep the old hours. That is a real cost. Phasing answers it: the plan reaches more districts in 2026, and it guards against spending a full year's budget on a result that may belong to one district.
