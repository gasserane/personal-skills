# Indicator set: youth contraception access, three peri-urban districts

**Skip to:** [Summary](#summary) · [Indicators](#the-six-indicators) · [Gaps](#data-gaps-to-settle-before-you-submit) · [Sources](#evidence-base)

## Summary

Use six indicators: four outputs (what the project delivers), measured every six months from your registers and tally sheets, and two outcomes (changes in young people), measured only at baseline and endline. The donor's outcome statement has two parts, "use" and "the method they choose", so each part gets its own outcome indicator.

- **Keep your existing indicator, with one fix.** Count individual young people, not visits. Paper registers count visits unless you add a simple client code.
- **Outcomes arrive twice, not every six months.** With no third survey, outcome indicators report at baseline and endline only. The three mid-project reports show outputs and one "choice" proxy from the registers.
- **Five of six indicators are project-specific.** No global standard exists for delivery measures such as "peer referrers active". Details are in the tier note below.
- **Four gaps need decisions before submission**: survey sample size, the session schedule, disability data in registers, and targets. They are listed at the end.
- **Change between surveys is not proof the project caused it.** There is no comparison group, so state this in the proposal.

## The six indicators

### 1. Number of young people aged 15-24 receiving contraceptive services (your existing indicator)
- **Level and tier:** Output. Tier 3 (project-specific; I found no matching global indicator).
- **Definition:** Counts individual young people aged 15-24 who had at least one contraceptive service (counselling or method supply) at the three clinics in the six-month period.
- **Numerator and denominator:** A count. For reach, divide by about 12,000, your estimate of the young population. I could not check that figure.
- **Disaggregation:** Age (15-17, 18-19, 20-24), sex, district and clinic, new or returning client, and session type (youth session or regular hours). Session type tells the team whether the new sessions bring in new clients.
- **Reliability: medium.** Monthly paper summaries cannot remove repeat visitors. Add a short client code to each register line. Report visits and unique young people side by side in the first period, so the series does not break.
- **How the team uses it:** Compare youth-session numbers with regular-hours numbers each month. If an evening session is nearly empty, move it.

### 2. Proportion of trained peer referrers who made at least one referral in the last three months
- **Level and tier:** Output. Tier 3.
- **Definition:** Measures how many of the 30 trained community health workers (CHWs) are actually active, not only trained.
- **Numerator and denominator:** CHWs with at least one referral slip issued in the last three months, divided by 30.
- **Disaggregation:** District, CHW sex and age band.
- **Reliability: medium-high.** The group is small and each CHW can be checked by name. Slips are issued by the CHW, so counts depend on honest tallying.
- **How the team uses it:** A fall below the first-period level shows which CHWs need support or replacement.

### 3. Proportion of peer referrals that led to a clinic visit within 30 days
- **Level and tier:** Output. Tier 3.
- **Definition:** Measures how many young people sent by a peer referrer reached a clinic soon afterwards.
- **Numerator and denominator:** Referral slips with a matching register entry within 30 days, divided by all slips issued. The 30-day window is my choice; fix it at the start and keep it.
- **Disaggregation:** Age band, sex, district, referring CHW.
- **Reliability: low-medium.** Treat the result as a minimum. Young people who attend without the slip, or at another clinic, are missed. Add a "referred by a peer" box to the register.
- **How the team uses it:** A low rate in one district points to a barrier (cost, distance, opening hours) that the team can investigate.

### 4. Proportion of young clients who received the method they asked for
- **Level and tier:** Output (service quality). Tier 3. This gives a six-monthly signal on "the method they choose" from routine data.
- **Definition:** Measures how often a young client who asked for a specific method left with that method.
- **Numerator and denominator:** Clients who received the requested method, divided by clients who asked for a specific method. Clients who came for advice only are excluded.
- **Disaggregation:** Age band, sex, district, method requested, and reason for any mismatch (stock-out, provider advice, age rule, client changed their mind).
- **Reliability: medium-low.** Staff must record the request before the consultation ends. If they fill both columns at the end, the two always match. Review a sample of register pages each quarter.
- **How the team uses it:** The mismatch reasons show whether the fix is stock, provider attitudes or rules.

### 5. Proportion of sexually active young people who do not want a pregnancy and use a modern method
- **Level and tier:** Outcome (change in the young people served). Tier 2: adapted from the SDG indicator on need for family planning satisfied with modern methods (3.7.1).
- **Definition:** Measures current modern-method use among young people who are having sex and are not trying to become pregnant.
- **Numerator and denominator:** Respondents using a modern method, divided by all respondents aged 15-24 who had sex in the past three months and are not trying to become pregnant. The three-month window is my choice. Fix it at baseline and keep it at endline.
- **Adaptation from the original:** The SDG indicator covers women aged 15-49 and is mainly built on married or in-union women. This version covers ages 15-24, unmarried young people, and young men (reporting own or partner's use). Results are not directly comparable with national SDG figures.
- **Disaggregation:** Age band, sex, district, marital status, disability (Washington Group Short Set, WG-SS), and in or out of school.
- **Reliability: medium-high.** Reporting of sexual activity is sensitive and is often understated, especially by unmarried adolescents. Interview each respondent alone, with interviewers of the same sex where possible.
- **How the team uses it:** Compare district results at endline to see where the project worked least.

### 6. Proportion of young modern-method users who say it is the method they wanted
- **Level and tier:** Outcome. Tier 3. A related published measure may exist (the Demographic and Health Surveys' Method Information Index, which I have not checked). Look at it before you fix the question wording.
- **Definition:** Measures whether young people using a method chose it themselves.
- **Numerator and denominator:** Users who answer "yes, it is the method I wanted", divided by all current modern-method users in the survey. Add one companion question: "Did you want a method you could not get?"
- **Disaggregation:** As indicator 5, plus the method used.
- **Reliability: medium.** Satisfaction answers lean positive, and the number of users may be small at baseline. Report counts alongside percentages.
- **How the team uses it:** Mainly for the endline lesson. It tells you whether more use came with more choice.

## Equity note

**What the set tracks:** Age band by sex by district, in the registers (indicators 1, 3, 4). A 15-year-old girl and a 23-year-old man face different barriers, and these three indicators can show both. The survey can compare age by sex, at a coarser level.

**What it does not track:**
- **Disability by age, and unmarried 15-17-year-old girls by district.** Survey subgroups of this kind will be too small. The numbers do not support a claim.
- **LGBTQI+ young people.** Asking about sexual orientation or gender identity in a household survey can put respondents at risk. I left it out and flag it as a gap.
- **Consent rules for under-18s.** These depend on the country, which you did not name. Check them before the survey and before recording minors in registers.

## Measurement mechanism

- **Clinic staff** keep the registers. Add columns for client code, age, sex, method asked for, method given, and "referred by a peer".
- **CHWs** keep a tally sheet and a numbered referral slip book. The programme officer collects both monthly.
- **The MA's MEL officer** compiles indicators 1 to 4 for each six-month report. The officer reviews a sample of register pages each quarter, and any gaps in method-requested entries are corrected then.
- **A survey team** runs the baseline and endline survey with the same questions. Interviewers need training in privacy and referral for anyone who discloses abuse or coercion.
- **Burden:** Indicators 1 to 4 add a few columns and one slip book. The survey is the main cost and is already budgeted.

## Tier distribution

Tier 1: none. Tier 2: one (indicator 5). Tier 3: five (indicators 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6). That is above the 20% flag in the standard. The four output indicators (1 to 4) measure this project's own delivery, where no global standard exists. ## Data gaps to settle before you submit

⚠️ Data gap: survey sample size for indicators 5 and 6 — not designed here, and 12,000 young people with sensitive questions may need more than a basic sample to detect a change — ask the evaluator or statistician to calculate it before the budget is fixed.

⚠️ Data gap: session schedule — "one evening and Saturday session per week" could mean one session per clinic or two — confirm, because it changes the expected count in indicator 1.

⚠️ Data gap: disability in clinic registers — a full Washington Group question set may be too long for a consultation, and for ages 5-17 a separate child module exists that I have not checked — use WG-SS in the survey, and decide whether to ask a short screen in clinics or descope it from the registers.

⚠️ Data gap: targets — no baseline exists for any indicator — set targets after the baseline survey and the first six months of registers, not before.
