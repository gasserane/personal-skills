**Main pathway:** Peer educators, mobile clinic days, respectful-care training and sessions with partners and mothers-in-law should together make contraception reachable, respectful and socially permitted, so young women can decide for themselves. The riskiest assumption is that partners and mothers-in-law will give up control over these decisions after monthly sessions, when we have not yet mapped who actually holds that power.

**Assumptions we made about missing inputs:** the 40 interviews are the only evidence on need. "Reach" means a young woman attends a service or session at least once. The MA has a licensed clinical team for the mobile days. Staff should confirm all three.

## Vision

By the end of year 3, young women aged 15-24 in the two settlements decide for themselves whether and when to use contraception, and get it without shame or third-party permission.

## Context and population

About 9,000 young women live in the two settlements. Many left school early, work informally, and rely on a partner or relative for money and permission to travel. The nearest public clinic is a 40-minute walk or a costly bus ride. Clinic staff are often unkind to unmarried young women.

The project cannot change national law or fund transport. It therefore brings services into the settlements and works on attitudes at clinics and at home. The needs assessment is 40 interviews, which suggests problems but cannot show how common they are.

## Pathway (read left to right)

1. **By month 12 (short term):** 24 peer educators are trained and active. Both mobile clinics run weekly. Staff at the two clinics finish respectful-care training. Partner and mother-in-law sessions are running.
2. **Years 2-3 (medium term):** Young women know their options and trust the mobile clinic. They attend, and clinic staff treat them respectfully. Partners and mothers-in-law become less opposed.
3. **End of year 3 (long term):** Young women choose and obtain contraception without shame or permission.

A precondition is a change that must happen first. Each step below is a change, not an activity.

## Causal claims and assumptions

| Link | Why we think it works | Assumption (outside our control) | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peer educators → trust and knowledge | Peers in the same settlement are more credible than outsiders | Peers are trusted, not seen as gossips, and keep confidences | Plausible: peer-support logic. Untested here |
| Mobile clinic → use of services | Removing the 40-minute walk removes the main barrier | A weekly day is enough, and young women can leave home without permission on that day | ⚠️ Untested mechanism: permission to leave home may block access even when the clinic is close |
| Respectful-care training → respectful treatment | Staff who understand the harm change their behaviour | Training changes behaviour, and clinic managers back it | ⚠️ Untested mechanism: training alone often does not change daily practice |
| Partner and mother-in-law sessions → less opposition | Information reduces fear and myths | These people attend, and they hold the real decision power | ⚠️ Untested mechanism: no power analysis exists |
| Services and less opposition → self-determined choice | Access plus social permission allows choice | Fear of violence or lost money does not override both | ⚠️ Untested mechanism |

## Who gains, who loses (power analysis)

⚠️ Feminist political economy analysis missing. We cannot name who holds power over these decisions. We can name likely actors to check:

- **Partners and mothers-in-law.** They may lose control over fertility and household money. Some may resist, or punish a young woman who attends. Invitation to sessions may not reach the people who decide.
- **Clinic staff and managers.** Unkind treatment may reflect heavy workloads or moral views. A course may be read as criticism.
- **Young women.** The 40 interviews defined the need. Out-of-school, working, very young (15-17) and unmarried women may be under-represented. Nobody has yet asked them to test this ToC.

**Example:** Ask 12 young women in a facilitated group who decides when they may leave home. If the answer is "my partner's mother", the sessions must reach her first.

## Threats to this ToC (rival explanations)

Contraceptive use could rise without us. Other NGOs, a national campaign, a new public clinic, social media or a drop in clinic fees could all raise it. A fall could come from stock-outs, a price rise or a local backlash. We will list other actors in the settlements at project start and ask clinics about supply.

**Contribution question:** To what extent and in what ways did the project contribute to young women's self-determined contraceptive choice, given other contributions and context?

## Plan to test the riskiest assumptions

Tests run in order of risk. We state in advance what would count as a failure.

| Assumption | Confirming evidence | Disconfirming evidence | Data source | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Partners and mothers-in-law decide, and will attend and shift | Attendance by decision-makers; users report fewer permission barriers | Low attendance by the real decision-makers; reports of conflict or violence after sessions | Power-mapping group discussions at month 2; session logs; short exit interviews | Month 2, month 6, month 12 |
| 2. Mobile clinic overcomes permission barriers | Repeat visits by unmarried and out-of-school women | Visits only by married or already-using women; permission still a stated barrier | Clinic registers by age and marital status; peer-educator notes | Month 6, month 12 |
| 3. Respectful-care training changes behaviour | Young women rate treatment as respectful; managers apply the new practice | Ratings do not change; staff report no managerial support | Exit questions at clinics (anonymous); observation with consent; manager interviews | Month 9, month 12 |
| 4. Peer educators are trusted | Women name peers as their first contact | Women avoid peers; confidentiality complaints | Peer-educator records; short group discussions | Month 6 |

**Decision rule:** If test 1 shows decision-makers are not attending or reacting badly, pause and redesign the sessions before year 2. If test 2 fails, move the clinic times or location with women's advice. At month 12 we revise this ToC in a short workshop with peer educators, clinic staff and young women.

## Data gaps

⚠️ Data gap: no analysis of who holds power over decisions — it decides whether sessions reach the right people — run a power-mapping exercise with young women in month 2.
⚠️ Data gap: no baseline of current contraceptive use or treatment at clinics — we cannot show change — collect a baseline survey in the first three months.
⚠️ Data gap: voices of the youngest, unmarried and out-of-school women are missing — outcomes may suit only some women — recruit them for the month 2 groups.
⚠️ Data gap: no mapping of other programmes in the settlements — rival explanations stay untested — list them at project start.

## What this ToC cannot tell you

1. **Most links are hypotheses.** Four of five causal links carry no named mechanism or tested evidence, so this is a plan to test, not a finding.
2. **No one has tested this draft.** Young women, peer educators, clinic staff and partners have not reviewed it. Do that before submission if possible.
3. **Some groups were not heard.** The 40 interviews may miss the youngest, the most isolated and women with disabilities.
4. **It may not travel.** The pathway rests on two settlements in one city and on the current legal and clinic setting. A change in national rules or clinic staffing could break it.
5. **Evidence collection carries risk.** Questions on contraception, partner control or violence can put a young woman in danger if overheard. Use private spaces, informed consent, no names in records, a referral route for anyone reporting violence, and safe-contact rules.
