# Working theory of change: contraception access and choice for young women in two informal settlements

**Verdict:** The project can plausibly contribute to the long-term outcome, but only if it changes three things together: how clinics treat young women, how partners and relatives behave, and how safely young women can reach a method. The weakest link is the partner and relative sessions, because we do not yet know who actually holds power over these decisions.

## Vision

By the end of year 3, young women aged 15-24 in the two settlements say they decide for themselves whether and when to use contraception. They can get a method without shame or anyone else's permission.

⚠️ Data gap: no baseline or target figures exist. Set them in the first three months, or the vision cannot be checked.

## Context

About 9,000 young women live in the two settlements. Many left school early, work informally, and depend on a partner or relative for money and permission to travel. The nearest public clinic is a 40-minute walk or a costly bus ride. Staff are often unkind to unmarried young women who ask for contraception. The MA has interviews with 40 young women and no earlier theory of change.

The project cannot change national law or fund transport. The pathway therefore brings services to the settlements and improves the clinics that remain.

## Main pathway (built backward from the vision)

**Long-term outcome (year 3 and after):** Young women decide for themselves and get contraception without shame or third-party permission.

**Medium-term changes (by end of year 3):**
- A. Young women use the mobile clinic and the two public clinics, and are treated with respect.
- B. Partners and mothers-in-law stop blocking or approving young women's choices.
- C. Young women feel able to decide and to ask for what they want.

**Short-term changes (by month 12):**
- 24 peer educators are trained and trusted by young women.
- Both weekly mobile clinic days run reliably.
- Clinic staff at both clinics finish the respectful-care course.
- The first rounds of partner and mothers-in-law sessions are held.

**How the links work:**
1. Trusted peers lower fear and give accurate information, so young women dare to come (short term to A and C).
2. Mobile days remove the 40-minute walk and the bus fare.
3. Staff training removes the unkind treatment that drives young women away from the clinics.
4. Sessions with partners and relatives loosen control over money, travel and permission (B).
5. A, B and C together produce the vision.

## Causal claims, assumptions and evidence status

| From → To | Why we think it works | Assumption (outside our control) | Evidence status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Peer educators → young women's confidence | Young women trust peers more than outsiders | Peers are seen as trustworthy and not as gossips | Plausible; untested here |
| Mobile days → use of methods | Removes distance and cost | Young women can leave home and be seen at the site without penalty | Untested |
| Clinic training → respectful care | Staff learn and apply new behaviour | Training changes behaviour, not just knowledge, and staff stay in post | ⚠️ Untested mechanism |
| Partner and relative sessions → less control | Informed relatives stop blocking | Those who attend are the ones who hold control | ⚠️ Untested mechanism |
| Respect, access and less control → own decision | Barriers fall together | Supply of methods does not run out | Plausible |

## Whose power, whose interests

⚠️ Feminist political economy analysis missing: we have not mapped who holds power over these young women's decisions. We can name the risks:

- Partners and relatives lose control, so they may resist or retaliate.
- Young women who change visibly may face violence or gossip.
- Peer educators are young women in the same settlements and carry the same risks.
- The 40 interviews came from young women only, so the sessions' design has no partner or relative voice yet.
- Unmarried young women, young mothers, and those fully dependent on a partner probably face different barriers. We have not tested whether one pathway fits all of them.

**Do-no-harm flag:** Sessions that announce who uses contraception can expose young women. Run a safeguarding check before the sessions start.

## Threats to this theory

Other things could produce the same change without us:
- Other NGOs or a government campaign operating in the settlements.
- Method stock-outs that make results look worse than the project deserves.
- People moving in and out of the settlements, so the 9,000 change over three years.
- Changes in women's income or schooling.

There is no comparison group, so we cannot claim the project caused the change. We can build a credible case that it contributed.

## Contribution question

To what extent and in what ways did the project contribute to young women deciding for themselves about contraception and getting it without shame or permission, given other contributions and the context?

## Evidence plan: test the four riskiest assumptions first

**First step (months 1-3):** Map who holds power over young women's decisions. Interview about 15 to 20 young women of different situations, plus some partners, relatives and clinic staff. Use the result to adjust the sessions before they scale.

| Assumption | Confirming evidence | Disconfirming evidence | Data source | Revisit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Those who attend sessions hold the control | Young women name attendees as their decision-makers | They name other people (for example a father, an employer) | Power-mapping interviews; attendance lists | Month 3, month 12 |
| Training changes clinic behaviour | Young women report respectful treatment; observers see it | Reports of the same unkindness; trained staff leave | Short exit interviews at clinics; supervisor observation | Month 6, month 12 |
| Young women can safely reach and be seen at mobile days | Steady attendance, including unmarried and dependent women | Attendance only from women with permission; reports of backlash | Attendance by group; safe reporting channel | Month 6, month 12 |
| Peer information leads to action | Women who met peers go on to seek methods | Knowledge rises, use does not | Short follow-up questions at the mobile clinic | Month 12, end of year 3 |

**How we will revisit the theory:** At month 12 and the end of year 3, programme staff and peer educators meet to compare results with the table above. They change or drop any link the evidence does not support.

## Data gaps

- ⚠️ Data gap: no baseline for contraceptive use, treatment at clinics or decision-making. Without it we cannot say what changed.
- ⚠️ Data gap: no power analysis. Link 4 rests on an assumption.
- ⚠️ Data gap: the 40 interviews have not been checked against this theory. Re-read them for evidence on shame, permission and cost.
- ⚠️ Data gap: no information on the supply of methods or stock-outs.
